How I work

Three principles I bring to every change

Design for the frontline

If it doesn't work for the person at the checkout or on the shift, it doesn't work. That's why I pushed for multilingual training materials, a first for Woolworths.

Change without stopping the operation

Stores, customers and teams keep going while the change happens. I plan readiness, training and support around that reality.

Honest about what works

I measure adoption, say plainly what didn't land, and fix it. The same goes for AI: I check its work before anyone acts on it.

What I am, and what I'm not

I'm a change leader, not a developer. I use AI to build and test ideas quickly, then do the part I'm best at: helping people adopt new ways of working.

2. From smart meter data to a habit that sticks

Data analysis · Behaviour change

After moving into a Sydney apartment I'd been put on a demand tariff: one 30-minute spike between 2 and 8 pm on a weekday sets a charge for the whole month. Working with an AI assistant, I analysed nine months of my half-hourly smart meter data to find what was driving the bill, then turned the findings into something easy to follow.

13,000+ Half-hourly readings across 274 days
~7× Overnight usage spike in a week of wet weather, likely the heating
18.6% Lower peak demand on the next bill
~$90/yr Modelled saving from switching to a time-of-use plan
What I did

Profiled baseload, hunted anomalies, and costed what the dishwasher and washer/dryer added when run in the peak window.

What I made

A one-page appliance cheat sheet, a slide pack, and a repeatable set of five questions anyone can ask AI about their own meter data.

The change lesson

Analysis doesn't change behaviour. A clear, simple rule on the fridge does. Same principle as any frontline rollout.

[Chart: normal nights vs the August spike]

The template: five questions anyone can ask AI about their meter data

  1. Is the data healthy?
  2. What is my baseload?
  3. Where are the spikes?
  4. Does anything look upside down?
  5. What should I change first?

3. A transport app built for one real user

Building with AI · Live data

Checking train times meant opening an app, typing destinations, and scanning results built for everyone. My journeys are the same handful every week. So, working with Claude and with no prior Python, I built a web app that shows only my routes, live, at a glance.

16 Pre-set routes, each with a to-home and away-from-home view
3 Transport modes: train, bus and light rail
Live Delays, countdowns and alerts from the TfNSW Open Data API
Designed around me

The stops I actually use, the right light rail line for game days, interchange details and boarding advice, in the order I need them.

What didn't work

Train fleet data was blocked by my free hosting, so we worked out the train type from carriage count instead. Journey tracking is still in progress.

The change lesson

Designing for one real user with a specific problem gets you something useful faster than designing for everyone.
